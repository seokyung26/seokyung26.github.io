<section id="greenhouse" class="research-panel" aria-labelledby="tab-greenhouse">
<header class="study-header"><p class="study-number">1. Greenhouse mapping</p><h2>Greenhouse microclimate mapping using a quadruped robot to assess fixed sensor representativeness</h2><p class="study-authors">HyeonJun Hwang<sup>a,†</sup>, <strong>Seokyung Park<sup>a,†</sup></strong> and Jaesung Park<sup>a,b,c,*</sup></p><p class="study-author-note">† Equal contribution. * Corresponding author: Jaesung Park.</p><details class="study-affiliations"><summary>Author affiliations</summary><p><sup>a</sup> Department of Bio-Industrial Machinery Engineering, College of Natural Resources and Life Science, Pusan National University, Miryang, Republic of Korea.<br><sup>b</sup> Department of Natural Resources Systems Engineering, Pusan National University, Yangsan, Republic of Korea.<br><sup>c</sup> Life and Industry Convergence Research Institute, Pusan National University, Miryang, Republic of Korea.</p></details><p><strong>Status:</strong> Manuscript in final preparation before submission.</p></header>
<p>This study examines how well fixed greenhouse sensors represent the microclimate across crop rows and canopy heights. A quadruped robot measured temperature, relative humidity and CO₂ to map spatial variation and compare mobile observations with fixed-sensor references.</p>
<h3>Materials &amp; Methods</h3>
<p>The experiment took place in a cherry tomato glasshouse in Miryang, Republic of Korea. The robot surveyed eight 54 m pipe-rail lanes: L1–L4 beside dense canopies and L5–L8 beside pruned canopies. The two canopy conditions provided contrasting environments for interpreting the mapped measurements.</p>
<figure class="research-figure canopy-panels canopy-triptych"><a class="canopy-panel-row" href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="View original Figure 1"><svg class="canopy-excerpt" viewBox="510 4 245 400" role="img" aria-labelledby="canopy-b-title" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><title id="canopy-b-title">Figure 1b: Dense-canopy section, L1–L4</title><image href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" width="1010" height="857"/></svg><svg class="canopy-excerpt" viewBox="760 4 245 400" role="img" aria-labelledby="canopy-c-title" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><title id="canopy-c-title">Figure 1c: Pruned-canopy section, L5–L8</title><image href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" width="1010" height="857"/></svg><svg class="canopy-excerpt" viewBox="0 408 1010 449" role="img" aria-labelledby="canopy-d-title" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><title id="canopy-d-title">Figure 1d: Greenhouse layout and fixed sensor locations</title><image href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" width="1010" height="857"/></svg></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 1b–d.</strong> Dense-canopy section (b; L1–L4), pruned-canopy section (c; L5–L8) and greenhouse layout with fixed sensor locations (d). <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View original figure ↗</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>A Unitree Go2 EDU carried identical environmental sensing modules at the <strong>fruit zone (1.15 m)</strong> and <strong>upper canopy (1.65 m)</strong>. Each module combined a BME280 temperature/humidity sensor and a CozIR-LP2 CO₂ sensor. An I²C multiplexer, Arduino Pro Mini and USB-to-TTL interface connected the two modules to the acquisition computer. Environmental readings were recorded at 2 Hz per height.</p>
<figure class="research-figure"><a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure2.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure2.png" alt="Quadruped platform, environmental sensor modules and data acquisition hardware" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 2.</strong> Mobile sensing platform with two measurement heights (a), environmental sensor module (b) and data acquisition module (c). <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure2.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View full size ↗</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>LiDAR-based rail detection and PID steering guided the forward measurement runs at <strong>0.5 m/s</strong>. ROS 2 recorded the environmental and robot data against a common clock. Four sessions—morning, midday, afternoon and evening—were conducted once each on separate days between 9 and 12 June 2026.</p>
<p>Sensor cross-calibration aligned the two modules, and fixed-sensor time series were used to correct temporal variation during sequential lane surveys. Observations were averaged into <strong>160 measured cells per map</strong> (eight lanes × 20 along-lane intervals), yielding <strong>24 maps</strong> across three variables, two heights and four sessions. Along-lane coordinates were estimated from elapsed traversal time and lane length; no spatial interpolation was used to fill the maps.</p>
<h3>Results</h3>
<figure class="research-figure"><a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure5.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure5.png" alt="Twenty-four spatial maps of temperature, humidity and carbon dioxide at two canopy heights across four sessions" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 5.</strong> Spatial distributions across eight lanes and twenty intervals per lane. Rows compare variables and heights; columns show the four sessions, which were conducted on separate days. <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure5.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View full size ↗</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>Spatial variation was consistently structured.</strong> All 24 maps showed positive spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I <strong>0.40–0.85; p &lt; 0.001</strong>). Upper-canopy relative humidity exceeded fruit-zone values by <strong>2.6–4.4 percentage points</strong>, and CO₂ was <strong>12–69 ppm</strong> higher, in every session. At the upper canopy, the pruned section was warmer and drier than the dense section; canopy condition and location were confounded, so the difference cannot be attributed to pruning alone.</p>
<figure class="research-figure"><a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure6.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure6.jpg" alt="Midday temperature humidity and carbon dioxide measurements overlaid on greenhouse LiDAR geometry" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 6.</strong> Midday temperature (a), relative humidity (b) and CO₂ (c) at the upper canopy and fruit zone, displayed against the 4D LiDAR point cloud. <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure6.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View full size ↗</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>The spatial overlay connects environmental readings with crop-row geometry.</strong> Figure 6 locates the two-height measurements within the greenhouse structure and makes differences between lanes and canopy levels easier to interpret. Measurement positions are relative lane coordinates reconstructed from traversal time, rather than independently validated absolute positions.</p>
<p><strong>Fixed readings did not fully represent the mobile spatial distributions.</strong> Humidity references were <strong>2.4–7.3 percentage points</strong> below mobile means; CO₂ references were <strong>48–116 ppm</strong> lower except in the evening. Depending on the session, 19–53% of cells differed from the fixed temperature reference by more than 0.5 °C, and 18–76% differed from the humidity reference by more than 5 percentage points.</p>
<p>The results support periodic mobile surveys to assess fixed-sensor representativeness and identify locations needing closer monitoring. They do not establish absolute sensor accuracy, energy savings or yield effects. Because the four sessions occurred on different days, session differences reflect both time of day and day-specific conditions.</p>
<h3>My contribution</h3><p>As a co-first author, I contributed to the methodology, experimental investigation and manuscript preparation.</p>

<h2>3. Planned plant phenotyping</h2>
<p><strong>Status:</strong> Planned research; results are not yet available.</p>
<p>The next direction is quadruped-based plant phenotyping: extending mobile observation from the surrounding environment to crop traits. The intended research connection is to relate plant observations to their environmental context. The experimental design, sensing configuration and target traits will be documented as the project develops.</p>
