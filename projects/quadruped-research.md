<section id="greenhouse" class="research-panel" aria-labelledby="tab-greenhouse">
<header class="study-header"><p class="study-number">1. Greenhouse mapping</p><h2>Greenhouse microclimate mapping using a quadruped robot to assess fixed sensor representativeness</h2><p class="study-authors">HyeonJun Hwang<sup>†</sup>, <strong>Seokyung Park<sup>†</sup></strong> and Jaesung Park<sup>*</sup></p><p class="study-author-note">† Equal contribution. * Corresponding author: Jaesung Park.</p><p class="study-status"><strong>Status:</strong> Manuscript in final preparation before submission.</p><p><strong>Conference presentation:</strong> Park, S. (2026). <em>Height-resolved 3D microclimate mapping and spatiotemporal analysis within greenhouse crop canopies using a quadruped robot.</em> AKABFE Student Research Spotlight, ASABE Annual International Meeting, Indianapolis, USA.</p></header>
<p>This study examines how well fixed greenhouse sensors represent the microclimate across crop rows and canopy heights. A quadruped robot measured temperature, relative humidity and CO₂ to map spatial variation and compare mobile observations with fixed-sensor references.</p>
<h3>Materials &amp; Methods</h3>
<p>The experiment took place in a cherry tomato glasshouse in Miryang, Republic of Korea. The robot surveyed eight 54 m pipe-rail lanes: L1–L4 beside dense canopies and L5–L8 beside pruned canopies. The two canopy conditions provided contrasting environments for interpreting the mapped measurements.</p>
<figure class="research-figure canopy-panels canopy-triptych"><a class="canopy-panel-row" href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="View original Figure 1"><svg class="canopy-excerpt" viewBox="510 4 245 400" role="img" aria-labelledby="canopy-b-title" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><title id="canopy-b-title">Figure 1b: Dense-canopy section, L1–L4</title><image href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" width="1010" height="857"/></svg><svg class="canopy-excerpt" viewBox="760 4 245 400" role="img" aria-labelledby="canopy-c-title" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><title id="canopy-c-title">Figure 1c: Pruned-canopy section, L5–L8</title><image href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" width="1010" height="857"/></svg><svg class="canopy-excerpt" viewBox="0 408 1010 449" role="img" aria-labelledby="canopy-d-title" xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg"><title id="canopy-d-title">Figure 1d: Greenhouse layout and fixed sensor locations</title><image href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" width="1010" height="857"/></svg></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 1b–d.</strong> Dense-canopy section (b; L1–L4), pruned-canopy section (c; L5–L8) and greenhouse layout with fixed sensor locations (d). <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure1.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View original figure ↗</a></figcaption></figure>
<p>A Unitree Go2 EDU carried identical environmental sensing modules at the <strong>fruit zone (1.15 m)</strong> and <strong>upper canopy (1.65 m)</strong>. Each module combined a BME280 temperature/humidity sensor and a CozIR-LP2 CO₂ sensor. An I²C multiplexer, Arduino Pro Mini and USB-to-TTL interface connected the two modules to the acquisition computer. Environmental readings were recorded at 2 Hz per height.</p>
<div class="greenhouse-platform-media"><figure class="research-figure"><a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure2.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure2.png" alt="Quadruped platform, environmental sensor modules and data acquisition hardware" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 2.</strong> Mobile sensing platform with two measurement heights (a), environmental sensor module (b) and data acquisition module (c). <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure2.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View full size ↗</a></figcaption></figure><figure class="greenhouse-video"><video controls muted playsinline loop preload="metadata" poster="/assets/images/greenhouse-mapping-video-poster.jpg" width="480" height="640" aria-label="Greenhouse mapping demonstration, without audio"><source src="/assets/videos/greenhouse-mapping-silent.mp4" type="video/mp4">Your browser does not support embedded video. <a href="/assets/videos/greenhouse-mapping-silent.mp4">Open the silent video</a>.</video><figcaption>Greenhouse mapping demonstration.</figcaption></figure></div>
<p>LiDAR-based rail detection and PID steering guided the forward measurement runs at <strong>0.5 m/s</strong>. ROS 2 recorded the environmental and robot data against a common clock. Four sessions—morning, midday, afternoon and evening—were conducted once each on separate days between 9 and 12 June 2026.</p>
<p>Sensor cross-calibration aligned the two modules, and fixed-sensor time series were used to correct temporal variation during sequential lane surveys. Observations were averaged into <strong>160 measured cells per map</strong> (eight lanes × 20 along-lane intervals), yielding <strong>24 maps</strong> across three variables, two heights and four sessions. Along-lane coordinates were estimated from elapsed traversal time and lane length; no spatial interpolation was used to fill the maps.</p>
<h3>Results</h3>
<figure class="research-figure"><a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure5.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure5.png" alt="Twenty-four spatial maps of temperature, humidity and carbon dioxide at two canopy heights across four sessions" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 5.</strong> Spatial distributions across eight lanes and twenty intervals per lane. Rows compare variables and heights; columns show the four sessions, which were conducted on separate days. <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure5.png" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View full size ↗</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>Spatial variation was consistently structured.</strong> All 24 maps showed positive spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I <strong>0.40–0.85; p &lt; 0.001</strong>). Upper-canopy relative humidity exceeded fruit-zone values by <strong>2.6–4.4 percentage points</strong>, and CO₂ was <strong>12–69 ppm</strong> higher, in every session. At the upper canopy, the pruned section was warmer and drier than the dense section; canopy condition and location were confounded, so the difference cannot be attributed to pruning alone.</p>
<figure class="research-figure"><a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure6.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><img src="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure6.jpg" alt="Midday temperature humidity and carbon dioxide measurements overlaid on greenhouse LiDAR geometry" loading="lazy"></a><figcaption><strong>Figure 6.</strong> Midday temperature (a), relative humidity (b) and CO₂ (c) at the upper canopy and fruit zone, displayed against the 4D LiDAR point cloud. <a href="/assets/images/greenhouse-v19-figure6.jpg" target="_blank" rel="noopener">View full size ↗</a></figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>The spatial overlay connects environmental readings with crop-row geometry.</strong> Figure 6 locates the two-height measurements within the greenhouse structure and makes differences between lanes and canopy levels easier to interpret. Measurement positions are relative lane coordinates reconstructed from traversal time, rather than independently validated absolute positions.</p>
<p><strong>Fixed readings did not fully represent the mobile spatial distributions.</strong> Humidity references were <strong>2.4–7.3 percentage points</strong> below mobile means; CO₂ references were <strong>48–116 ppm</strong> lower except in the evening. Depending on the session, 19–53% of cells differed from the fixed temperature reference by more than 0.5 °C, and 18–76% differed from the humidity reference by more than 5 percentage points.</p>
<p>The results support periodic mobile surveys to assess fixed-sensor representativeness and identify locations needing closer monitoring. They do not establish absolute sensor accuracy, energy savings or yield effects. Because the four sessions occurred on different days, session differences reflect both time of day and day-specific conditions.</p>
<h3>My contribution</h3><p>As a co-first author, I contributed to the methodology, experimental investigation and manuscript preparation.</p>

<header class="study-header"><p class="study-number">2. Reservoirs</p><h2>Quadruped-based Close-Range Upstream Surveys</h2><p class="study-status"><strong>Status:</strong> Completed.</p><p><strong>Commissioning institution:</strong> National Disaster Management Research Institute (NDMI), Republic of Korea.</p><p><strong>Contract research:</strong> Quadruped-based close-range soil surveys upstream of reservoirs, conducted under the project on integrated technology development for reservoir embankment risk prediction and maintenance management (Phase I).</p></header>
<p>A quadruped robot was used to collect close-range images of exposed soil in the upstream areas of agricultural reservoirs before and after rainfall. The aim is to document ground-surface changes associated with soil erosion and sediment deposition. To support comparisons at corresponding locations across survey dates, the platform combined <strong>RTK-GNSS positioning</strong> with <strong>continuous RGB imaging from an action camera</strong>.</p>
<p>The recording system logged frame timestamps alongside GNSS observations in ROS 2. During post-processing, GNSS coordinates were interpolated to the frame timestamps, and selected frames were extracted with location metadata and displayed along the recorded survey routes. This provides a location-linked image dataset for repeated observation and subsequent analysis of rainfall-related surface changes.</p>

<section id="phenotyping" class="research-panel" aria-labelledby="tab-phenotyping">
<header class="study-header"><p class="study-number">3. Plant phenotyping</p><h2>Quadruped-based 3D plant phenotyping</h2><p class="study-status"><strong>Status:</strong> Ongoing research.</p></header>
<p>I am developing a quadruped-based workflow for <strong>close-range 3D crop reconstruction</strong>. RGB-D observations collected along soybean rows are cleaned, pose-corrected and registered to build row-scale point clouds for plant phenotyping.</p>

<h3>Preliminary Result: Pod-level Depth and 3D Reconstruction</h3>
<p>This experiment extends the row-scale reconstruction workflow to individual soybean pods. <strong>SAM3</strong> was used to segment and track each plant and to remove leaf regions, <strong>PodNet</strong> generated pod instance masks in the original 1280 × 720 images, and <strong>DA3</strong> estimated high-resolution depth at an input resolution of 1008 pixels. Combining these models preserves pod-specific image detail while locating the detected pods in the robot-centred 3D scene.</p>
<div class="phenotyping-result"><div><p>The reconstruction contains <strong>31,760 filtered pod points</strong> across four plants. PodNet also detected pods on P6–P8 that were largely missed by SAM3 text prompting alone. The displayed plant-level totals are provisional 3D clusters, not validated pod counts, because the same pod can shift by several centimetres between viewpoints.</p></div><ol aria-label="Pod-level 3D reconstruction workflow"><li>SAM3 plant tracking and leaf-mask removal</li><li>PodNet instance segmentation of visible pods</li><li>DA3-1008 depth extraction within each pod mask</li><li>Robot-pose transformation and 3D fusion</li></ol></div>
<p>For every observation, only DA3 depth values inside the PodNet masks were retained and corrected using the station-specific depth scale. Sampled pod pixels were transformed with the robot pose, merged on a 3 mm grid and filtered to remove outliers. In the updated reconstruction, the leaf-removed plant point clouds are shown in grey and the PodNet pod points are coloured by plant.</p>
<p>The resulting depth maps reveal front-to-back variation among pods within dense clusters. In 3D, pods on P5 form a compact group near <em>x</em> ≈ 1.1 m, whereas those on P8 are distributed more broadly in height near <em>x</em> ≈ 1.6 m. Because a pod is only about 20–40 pixels wide in the source images, depth mixing at object boundaries can elongate some reconstructed points; this remains a limitation of close-range monocular depth estimation. PodNet was restored from the <a href="https://github.com/Boatsure/PodNet" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PodSeg/PodNet project</a> (Plant Phenomics, 2025).</p>

<h4>P5: SAM3-tracked Pods to Pod-specific Depth and 3D Point Clouds</h4>
<p>For plant P5, PodNet detections observed in at least two frames were used as prompts for a single SAM3 video-tracking session. SAM3 propagated each pod identity forward and backward through the 39-frame sequence, recovering masks in frames where PodNet did not produce a detection. Quality checks retained masks with plausible area, sufficient overlap with the P5 plant region and a 3D centre within 4 cm of the corresponding pod track.</p>
<p>After eroding each mask by two pixels, station-corrected DA3-1008 depth was retained near the frame-wise median to reduce boundary mixing. Multi-view points were aligned along the camera ray, refined to the clearest reference frame with ICP, merged on a 2 mm grid and filtered for outliers. This produced pod-specific 3D point clouds for <strong>24 consistently tracked pods</strong> and <strong>12,022 points</strong>. The median estimated pod length was <strong>4.2 cm</strong> (10th–90th percentile: 2.9–5.2 cm).</p>
